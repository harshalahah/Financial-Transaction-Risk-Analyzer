# Financial Transaction Risk Analyzer — Technical Documentation

Version 1.0 · Python 3.12 · FastAPI · scikit-learn · pandas

---

## 1. Problem framing

A retail bank processes tens of millions of transactions a day. A tiny fraction
are financial crime: card fraud, money laundering, mule activity. Two hard
constraints shape any system that tries to find them.

**Extreme class imbalance.** In this dataset 0.44% of transactions are
suspicious. A model that says "nothing is suspicious" scores 99.5% accuracy and
has zero value. Accuracy is the wrong metric and must not appear in the
evaluation.

**A finite alert budget.** Every alert costs an analyst's time. A bank might
be able to review 1% of daily volume. The question is therefore never "how
accurate is the model" but *"of the transactions we can afford to look at, how
much of the real crime do we capture, and can we justify each alert to a
regulator?"*

This project is built around those two constraints end to end: the data
generator injects realistic typologies *and* realistic false-positive traps,
the features are all customer-relative, the evaluation is ranking-based at a
fixed budget, and every alert carries a human-readable reason.

### Scope and honesty about the data

The transaction data is **synthetic**, generated by `src/generate_data.py`.
Labelled bank transaction data is confidential and no public dataset carries
the AML typologies this system targets. Generating it gives control over base
rates and lets the repo run anywhere. The trade-off, which should be stated
plainly rather than hidden: metrics on synthetic data measure whether the
pipeline can recover patterns that were deliberately planted, not real-world
performance. The generator was deliberately made harder after a first pass
scored a near-perfect PR-AUC of 1.000 — that result was a symptom of data that
was too easy, not of a good model (see §3.3).

The data is modelled on **Indian retail banking**: amounts in INR, UPI as the
dominant channel by count, IMPS/NEFT transfers, ATM and branch cash, and the
Rs 50,000 PAN requirement on cash deposits as the threshold structuring hides
under.

## 3. Component walkthrough

### 3.1 `generate_data.py` — the dataset

**Customer layer.** 1,200 customers, each with a home country, age, account
tenure, account type and a private `spend_scale` drawn from a lognormal. That
last field is the important one: it means Rs 40,000 is routine for one customer
and extraordinary for another, which forces the features to be *relative to the
customer* rather than absolute. A system that flags "amount > Rs 25,000" would be
useless here, exactly as it is in a real bank.

**Genuine behaviour.** Inter-arrival times come from an exponential
distribution, amounts from a lognormal around the customer's scale (median
transaction Rs 517), with an Indian channel mix — UPI 46% of transactions,
cards 28%, IMPS/NEFT 14%, ATM and branch cash the rest — and categories like
kirana, fuel, food delivery, recharges, gold and P2P transfers.

**Injected typologies** (≈6% of customers experience one):

| Typology | Pattern | Real-world analogue |
|---|---|---|
| `cnp_fraud` | small test transaction, then 4–8 escalating online purchases minutes apart, often foreign, often a new device | stolen card tested then drained |
| `structuring` | 3–6 cash deposits at branch/ATM sitting below the Rs 50,000 PAN limit, spread over days | smurfing to avoid furnishing a PAN and leaving a paper trail |
| `mule` | one large inbound transfer, then near-total outflow within hours to new beneficiaries abroad | money-mule pass-through account |

**Confounders — the part that makes the dataset honest.** Genuine transactions
also include, unlabelled: festival and gold purchases (2% of rows),
legitimate large cash deposits including some near the PAN limit (1.2%),
travel windows producing clustered foreign spending for 30% of customers, and occasional new devices. Without these the model could separate
classes on "foreign + large = fraud" and every metric would be meaningless.

Result: **70,814 transactions, 0.443% suspicious** (193 fraud, 77 structuring,
44 mule).

### 3.2 `features.py` — feature engineering

The governing rule, stated at the top of the module: *every feature must be
computable at the instant the transaction arrives, from that transaction plus
the customer's history strictly before it.* This single rule does two jobs — it
prevents target leakage, and it guarantees the training features and the live
API features are the same quantity.

**The 25 features, by family:**

| Family | Features | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Raw | `amount`, `log_amount`, `hour`, `day_of_week`, `is_night`, `is_weekend` | baseline signal; log tames a heavy right tail |
| Velocity | `txn_count_1h/24h/7d`, `amount_sum_24h/7d` | fraud bursts are a rate anomaly, not an amount anomaly |
| Deviation | `amount_zscore_cust`, `amount_ratio_to_cust_median` | compares the amount to *this* customer's own baseline |
| Recency | `seconds_since_prev_txn` | seconds-apart transactions are machine-driven |
| Novelty | `is_new_device_for_customer`, `is_new_country_for_customer`, `is_foreign_country`, `unique_countries_24h` | account-takeover and impossible-travel signals |
| Threshold | `pct_of_reporting_limit`, `near_reporting_limit` (Rs 50,000 PAN limit) | direct structuring cue |
| Context | `is_new_beneficiary`, `tenure_months`, `age` | mule accounts are young and pay strangers |
| Categorical | `channel`, `merchant_category`, `account_type` | one-hot encoded downstream |

**How leakage is avoided mechanically.** All rolling windows are computed on a
time index and then have the current row subtracted: `rolling("1h").count() - 1`
and `rolling("24h").sum() - amount`. The customer's mean, standard deviation and
median come from an `expanding()` window that is then `.shift(1)`-ed, so the
current transaction never contributes to the baseline it is being judged
against. `test_velocity_window_excludes_current_transaction` asserts this: a
customer's very first transaction must show `txn_count_1h == 0`.

**Training/serving parity.** `build_features` (vectorised, whole ledger) and
`build_features_online` (one transaction plus a history list) implement the
same definitions. Parity is not assumed — `test_online_and_batch_features_agree`
takes a real customer, splits their history, computes the vector both ways and
asserts they match. Training/serving skew is one of the most common ways a
production ML system quietly degrades, so it gets a test rather than a comment.

### 3.3 `train.py` — training and evaluation

**Time-based split, not random.** The earliest 70% of the timeline trains
(49,570 rows), the latest 30% tests (21,244 rows). A random split would let a
fraud burst have some of its transactions in train and the rest in test, which
inflates results, and it ignores that crime patterns drift. This mirrors how a
model would actually be deployed: fit on the past, judged on the future.

**Preprocessing** is a `ColumnTransformer` inside a `Pipeline` — median
imputation plus standardisation for numeric columns, most-frequent imputation
plus `OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore")` for categoricals. Keeping it in
the pipeline means the fitted scaler and encoder travel with the model in the
joblib bundle, so the API cannot drift from training-time preprocessing.
`handle_unknown="ignore"` matters in production: a new merchant category
appearing at 3 a.m. should not throw a 500.

**Three candidate models**, all with class imbalance handled:

| Model | Configuration | PR-AUC | ROC-AUC | Recall @1% budget |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Logistic regression | `class_weight="balanced"`, C=0.5 | 0.321 | 0.987 | 0.667 |
| **Random forest (selected)** | 300 trees, `balanced_subsample` | 0.828 | 0.999 | **0.978** |
| Gradient boosting | 250 trees, lr 0.08, depth 3 | 0.838 | 0.998 | 0.892 |

**The selection criterion is recall at the alert budget, not PR-AUC.** Gradient
boosting has the marginally better PR-AUC (0.838 vs 0.828) but the random forest
catches far more crime inside the 1% of volume we can actually staff (0.978 vs
0.892). PR-AUC summarises the whole curve; only one part of that curve is
reachable with the analysts available, so the model is picked on the part that
will be used. PR-AUC breaks ties.

The logistic baseline earns its place by showing the gap: the typologies are
interaction effects (foreign *and* new device *and* fast *and* large), which a
linear model cannot represent. Note also that ROC-AUC is ~0.99 for all three
and discriminates between them barely at all — on imbalanced data ROC-AUC is
flattered by the huge negative class. PR-AUC separates them cleanly (0.321 vs
0.838). That contrast is the main argument for reporting precision-recall.

**Threshold selection.** `predict()` at 0.5 is meaningless here. `pick_threshold`
walks the precision-recall curve and takes the lowest threshold that still holds
precision ≥ 0.50, maximising recall subject to that constraint. Selected
threshold: **0.1226**. In a real programme this constraint would come from the
investigations team's capacity, not from a developer.

**Final operating point on the held-out period:**

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Recall (suspicious) | 0.946 |
| Precision (suspicious) | 0.500 |
| Alert rate | 0.83% of transactions |
| Confusion matrix | TP 88 · FN 5 · FP 88 · TN 21,063 |

Per-typology recall — the check that matters for coverage, since an aggregate
number can hide a whole blind typology:

| Typology | Test cases | Recall |
|---|---|---|
| Card-not-present fraud | 63 | 0.968 |
| Mule pass-through | 11 | 0.909 |
| Structuring | 19 | 0.895 |

Structuring is the weakest, which is expected: it is defined by a *sequence* of
deposits rather than by any single transaction, so a per-transaction model sees
only part of the pattern. That is precisely the gap rule `R03_REPEAT_CASH_PATTERN`
covers in the service layer.

**Top features** (random-forest importance): `amount_ratio_to_cust_median`
(0.154), `amount` (0.113), `pct_of_reporting_limit` (0.097), `log_amount`
(0.091), `seconds_since_prev_txn` (0.073), `channel=CARD_ONLINE` (0.072). The
single most important feature is the engineered customer-relative one — the
amount compared to that customer's own median — ranking above raw amount. That
ordering is the quantitative case for the feature engineering work.

### 3.4 `service.py` — the decision layer

**Customer history store.** Behavioural features need the recent past.
`CustomerHistory` keeps a 200-transaction deque per customer, seeded from the
CSV at startup. It is deliberately behind a small interface with `get`/`add`,
because in production this is a feature store (Redis, Feast, or the bank's own
streaming platform) and swapping it should touch one class, not the feature code.

**Why a rule engine sits next to the model.** No bank ships a bare ML score.
Rules are deterministic, auditable and can be explained to a regulator in one
sentence; models generalise to patterns nobody wrote a rule for. This system
runs both and takes `max(model_score, rule_score)` — rules can only ever *raise*
risk, never suppress a model alert, so adding a rule can never silently create
a blind spot.

| Code | Trigger | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| `R01_VELOCITY_BURST` | ≥4 card-not-present transactions in an hour | 0.55 |
| `R02_STRUCTURING` | cash deposit 85–100% of the Rs 50,000 PAN limit | 0.45 |
| `R03_REPEAT_CASH_PATTERN` | ≥2 similar sub-PAN-threshold cash deposits in 7 days | 0.60 |
| `R04_AMOUNT_ANOMALY_NEW_DEVICE` | amount z-score ≥6 on an unseen device | 0.50 |
| `R05_HIGH_RISK_GEOGRAPHY` | large first-time transaction in a high-risk jurisdiction | 0.45 |
| `R06_NEW_BENEFICIARY_VALUE` | large crypto/P2P transfer to a first-time beneficiary | 0.35 |
| `R07_IMPOSSIBLE_TRAVEL` | 3+ countries in 24 hours | 0.40 |

**Banding.** `LOW → ALLOW`, `MEDIUM → REVIEW` (score ≥ threshold),
`HIGH → BLOCK` (score ≥ max(3× threshold, 0.5)). Three bands rather than a
binary flag because the business response differs: a review queue item and a
declined payment are different costs.

**Explanation by ablation.** For any non-LOW transaction, `_top_signals`
replaces one feature at a time with a neutral value and measures how far the
score drops. The largest drops are the features carrying the alert. This is a
cheap, model-agnostic local attribution — the same intuition as SHAP without
the dependency — and it produces output an investigator can read:
`seconds_since_prev_txn=240 (+0.95)` says "this fired because it came four
minutes after the last one".

Under RBI and FIU-IND expectations on transaction monitoring — and the same
principle in every jurisdiction HSBC operates in — an unexplainable
financial-crime decision is not deployable. Explanation is a requirement here,
not a nice-to-have.

### 3.5 `schemas.py` and `api.py` — the service

**Validation as the first line of defence.** `pydantic` v2 models enforce types,
`amount > 0`, ISO-8601 timestamps, two-letter country codes and a closed set of
channels, with country codes normalised to uppercase. A malformed payload gets
a 422 with a field-level error before any model code runs
(`test_validation_rejects_bad_payload`).

**Endpoints:**

| Method | Path | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| GET | `/health` | model loaded, version, threshold, uptime — what a load balancer polls |
| POST | `/score` | score one transaction; returns score, band, decision, rules, signals, latency |
| POST | `/score/batch` | up to 1,000 transactions per call for end-of-day sweeps |
| GET | `/metrics/model` | the deployed model's offline evaluation metrics |
| GET | `/customers/{id}` | the behavioural profile the model reasoned over |
| GET | `/docs` | OpenAPI UI, generated automatically |

**Production details that are easy to skip and worth keeping:** the model loads
once in a `lifespan` context manager rather than per request; every request gets
an `x-correlation-id` (accepted from the caller or generated) that flows into
structured logs, which is how you trace a disputed decision through a bank's
estate months later; a global exception handler returns a clean 500 instead of
a stack trace; measured latency is returned in the response body.

**Measured performance:** p50 **19.4 ms**, p95 22.4 ms per scored transaction
including feature computation, model inference, rules and explanation, single
process.

### 3.6 `tests/test_app.py`

Nine tests in three groups:

1. **Feature correctness** — no NaNs, expected columns present, and the leakage
   guard on rolling windows.
2. **Training/serving parity** — batch and online feature vectors must agree.
3. **API contract** — health, a normal transaction, a structuring transaction
   that must fire `R02`, a malformed payload that must 422, batch scoring, and
   the metrics endpoint.

All 9 pass. The two worth pointing at in an interview are the leakage guard and
the parity test, because they test the failure modes that are invisible until
production.

## 5. Known limitations

Stating these yourself is worth more than being caught out on them.

1. **Synthetic data.** Metrics show the pipeline recovers planted patterns; they
   are not an estimate of real-world performance.
2. **Per-transaction scoring.** Structuring and mule activity are really
   *sequence* problems. A sequence model (or entity-level aggregation over a
   customer-day) would fit them better; rules currently plug the gap.
3. **In-memory history.** Single-process and lost on restart. Production needs a
   shared feature store, which is why it sits behind an interface.
4. **No concept-drift monitoring.** Criminals adapt. A real deployment needs
   population-stability monitoring on feature distributions and scheduled
   retraining on analyst feedback.
5. **Fairness is unassessed.** Financial-crime models can produce disparate
   impact across demographics or nationalities. `country` and `age` are inputs;
   before any real use they would need a bias assessment. The right answer is
   not "the model doesn't see race" — proxies exist.
6. **50% precision means one false positive per true positive.** Acceptable for
   a review queue, unacceptable for automatic blocking. The BLOCK band would
   need a much higher bar in production.

## 6. Where it goes next

Ordered by value, not by novelty:

- **Graph features** — shared devices, beneficiaries and IPs across customers.
  Mule networks are a graph problem; per-account features see one node at a time.
- **Sequence model** — a GRU or transformer over the customer's last *n*
  transactions, which would address limitation 2 directly.
- **Feedback loop** — store analyst dispositions and retrain on confirmed
  outcomes rather than the original labels.
- **Drift monitoring** — PSI on feature distributions, alerting on shift.
- **Champion/challenger** — shadow-score a new model against the live one before
  promotion, standard practice in bank model governance.

## 7. Interview preparation

### 7.1 Why this project fits HSBC

HSBC runs one of the largest financial-crime compliance operations in banking,
and has invested heavily in AI for transaction monitoring and AML. This project
speaks to that directly, but the part that carries weight is not the ML — it is
that the design decisions are the ones a regulated bank actually cares about:
explainability, auditability, the alert budget, training/serving parity, and
honestly documented limitations.

### 7.2 Questions you should expect, with the answers this codebase gives

**"Your data is fake — what does that prove?"**
That the pipeline recovers planted patterns, and nothing more. I state that
in the documentation. What it does demonstrate is the engineering: leak-free
point-in-time features, time-based evaluation, threshold selection against an
alert budget, tested training/serving parity. Swap in real labelled data and
only the generator is discarded.

**"Why not accuracy?"**
At a 0.44% base rate, predicting "never suspicious" gives 99.5% accuracy and
catches nothing. I report PR-AUC, recall at a 1% alert budget, and the full
confusion matrix. I'd also point at ROC-AUC being ~0.99 for all three models
while PR-AUC ranges 0.321–0.838 — ROC-AUC is flattered by the negative class.

**"How do you know you have no leakage?"**
Structurally, every rolling window subtracts the current row and every
customer-baseline statistic is shifted by one. Empirically, a test asserts a
customer's first transaction has zero prior-hour activity, and a second test
asserts the offline and online feature paths produce identical vectors.

**"How would you explain a declined payment to a customer or a regulator?"**
Every alert returns the rules that fired in plain language plus the top
contributing features by ablation. For example: "four online transactions in
the last hour" plus `seconds_since_prev_txn=240 (+0.95)`.

**"Why the random forest?"**
I picked on the business objective rather than the headline metric. Gradient
boosting had a slightly better PR-AUC (0.838 vs 0.828), but the random forest
caught 97.8% of suspicious transactions inside the 1% alert budget against
89.2% — and the budget is the part of the curve we can actually staff. The
logistic baseline (0.321 PR-AUC) stayed in the comparison to show how much of
the signal is interaction effects a linear model can't represent.

**"How would you deploy this?"**
Containerised behind the existing payment rails, model loaded once at startup,
correlation IDs into structured logging, batch endpoint for overnight sweeps,
and a champion/challenger shadow deployment before any new model takes live
traffic. The in-memory history store would become a feature store.

**"What's wrong with it?"**
Section 5 — I'd lead with the synthetic data and the per-transaction framing
being the wrong shape for structuring, and the fact that fairness is unassessed
while `country` and `age` are model inputs.
